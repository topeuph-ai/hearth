Hearth in plain words

A record about a person, held by the people who look after them — and by nobody else.

For anybody who is not a programmer. Checked against the working app on 24 September 2026, version 0.3.4. Where something is not finished, it says so.

The short version

  • Hearth holds an "About Me": what matters to a person, how to talk with them, what to please do and please not do. It follows a national standard (PRSB About Me). Nothing medical — no medicines, no diagnoses.
  • The record lives on the devices of the people around the person — family, carers, a nurse. Each has a copy. There is no company or database in the middle.
  • Only the person, or whoever holds the record for them, can change it. Everybody else can read it, suggest things, and say "I have read this".
  • It is locked. Only people let into the circle can open it, and somebody taken out cannot open what is written after they left.
  • It keeps working when the internet goes, between devices that are already talking to each other.

The problem

Everybody wants the same information about a person, and nobody wants to be the one who holds it.

Take Margaret. She has dementia. A GP, a district nurse, a care agency, a day centre, a hospital ward and her daughter all look after her. Each writes things down in their own system, and those systems do not talk to each other. So Margaret, or her daughter, explains her again, every time: speak to her left side, she is deaf on the right; do not move her chair.

The obvious fix is one shared place for her record. But whoever runs that place becomes the operator — legally responsible for sensitive information about a vulnerable person, written by half a dozen organisations, and expected to still exist in twenty years. Almost nobody wants that job, and the ones who took it did not always keep it.

Hearth's answer: nobody holds it

The people around the person each keep a copy, and their devices share it with each other directly.

In Hearth, the record for Margaret is called a circle. Everybody Margaret — or her daughter, acting for her — lets in keeps a full copy on their own phone or computer. When anybody changes something, their device passes it straight to the others.

There is no server that holds the record, no database, no account and no company. So there is nothing central to buy, run, break into, be forced to hand over, or shut down.

Every device also checks the rules for itself — for example, "only Margaret's side may change Margaret's record". So even somebody with a tampered copy of the app cannot write what the rules forbid: every other device would refuse it.

A circle, and how you join

You ask to join; the person who holds the circle lets you in; your device gets its own copy.

Every circle has a holder: Margaret herself, or somebody she trusts to act for her, often a daughter or a case manager. The holder decides who is in.

Joining works like knocking at a door. The holder shares the circle's address — as a QR code to scan, or rows of letters to type. Anybody with the address can knock. Nobody gets in until the holder says yes. Having the address shows a stranger nothing about Margaret.

Who can do what

One person's words; everybody else's help.

The holder

  • Writes and changes the record
  • Decides who is in the circle, and can take people out
  • Accepts or sets aside suggestions
  • Can give a pass, and name a successor

Everybody in the circle

  • Reads the record, with photographs, recorded voice and short videos
  • Suggests something to add — "her allotment; she talked about it all summer"
  • Says "I have read this version"

"I have read this version" matters more than it sounds: a family can see that the new carer has actually read about Margaret, rather than hoping so. But it proves only that somebody said they read it. Anybody can call themselves "district nurse" — Hearth always shows that as what they said, never as checked.

Locked, and taking somebody out

The record is locked with a key only the circle has. Take somebody out, and the circle changes the key.

Everything in a circle — the record, suggestions, photographs, sound and video — is locked before it leaves anybody's device. A copy sitting on somebody's disk is unreadable without the circle's key, and only people let into the circle are given it.

When the holder takes somebody out, the circle makes a new key and gives it to everybody except that person. Whatever is written afterwards still reaches their device, like everything else — and cannot be opened there.

A pass, for somebody passing through

A nurse on a ward doesn't need to join. A pass lets them read chosen parts, for a set time.

Not everybody who helps Margaret should keep a copy of her record. A nurse on a hospital ward at two in the morning, a paramedic, a carer covering one shift — they need the basics, once. The holder can give them a pass: chosen parts of the record, for just today, a week, or until the holder stops it.

The pass is read from the holder's own device each time. Nothing is copied onto the reader's device, and the holder sees when it was used ("Ward 7 read this").

When the holder can't carry on

The holder can name a successor in advance. Taking over is slow and visible on purpose.

For people with dementia, the day the holder can no longer look after the circle is expected, not unusual. So the holder can name a successor, and a checker — somebody close enough to check on her in person. They are never the same person.

When the internet goes

Devices that are already talking keep sharing, even with the broadband unplugged.

Because nothing depends on a central server, two devices in the same house keep sharing when the internet goes down. This was shown on 14 September 2026 on a desktop and a laptop: the broadband was unplugged, the record was changed on one, and the change appeared on the other.

The catch: if both devices are switched off and on again with no internet, standard Holochain — the software Hearth is built on — cannot find the other device in the same room. A test version of Holochain that looks on the local network fixes that, and found the other machine in about a minute. It also made a two-minute video move quickly instead of taking ten minutes. That version is a field test, so the everyday download does not have it yet.

How it is different

Every other way to share a record names somebody to hold it. Hearth names nobody.

What it can't do

Said here rather than buried.

Holding somebody's device is holding the keys. The released app does not yet ask for a password when it starts. That is decided, and still to be built.

A stranger cannot find the record. A paramedic who has never heard of Hearth has no way to know it exists. Paper solved that with a sticker on the fridge; Hearth has not.

Somebody taken out keeps what they already read. Only what is written afterwards is protected.

It needs a device. Somebody with no phone or computer, and nobody to hold the circle for them, is not helped by this. Windows only, for now.

Nobody who does security for a living has checked it yet. Four AI reviews and an audit of our own found and fixed real problems. A human review is what it needs next.

Coming next, and finding out more

Being tested

Calls. Voice and video between two people in a circle, straight from device to device and never recorded. Off until the holder turns them on, so nobody gets calls they didn't expect.

Everything here can be checked. The code, the tests, and a page listing exactly what has and hasn't been shown are at github.com/topeuph-ai/hearth — start with "what is proven".

Hearth is free and open source (Apache 2.0). Built by one person whose field is music, not software: the code is written with AI assistance, and the design decisions are his.

It follows the PRSB About Me standard for what it holds. It is not a medical device and holds nothing clinical.
