    What people should know

    The things that matter most, in their own words — and the things anybody looking after them should know whatever else is happening.

    • What would somebody caring for them need to know in an emergency? Being seen somewhere quiet or dark, needing a translator, a food allergy, a risk of choking.
    • What matters to them as a person — their faith, their culture, their pets, the things they enjoy and the places they like to go.
    • What are they hoping for? People have goals at every age.
    • If anything is already written down elsewhere — an advance decision, a power of attorney, a hospital passport — say so here.

    Who is important to them, and why. Family, friends, neighbours, staff — and how they want those people involved.

    • Who are the important people, and what are they to them?
    • Who should be rung in an emergency?
    • Who should be asked about their care, day to day as well as in a crisis?
    • Is there anybody who should not be contacted? Only if they want to say. This is read by everybody in the circle.

    How they communicate, and how they would like people to communicate with them.

    • What do people need to know? Speaking slowly, speaking to one side, a hearing aid, signing, symbols, a preferred language.
    • How do they like to be addressed? First name, full name, something else.
    • How do they let somebody know they are in pain, anxious or upset? It is often not in words.
    • How do they say yes and no, and how do they make a choice?
    • How would they raise a worry, or say they are unhappy with something?

    What a good day looks like, what a bad day looks like, and what makes the difference.

    • On an ordinary day — what can they do, how do they feel, how are they with people?
    • On a bad day, what is different?
    • What tends to make them unwell, and what heads it off? Not drinking enough, too much noise, a missed routine.
    • What are the early signs that something is wrong?
    • Anything they live with day to day, and how they manage it.

    Written straight to whoever is reading it. The standard keeps this one in those words, and so does this.

    • Please do — “Talk to me, not to my daughter.” “Remind me to take my tablets.” “Tell me what is happening and why.”
    • Please do not — a subject to leave alone, an assumption people keep making, help given when it was not wanted.

    The practical part: what they do themselves, what they want a hand with, and how they want that hand given.

    • Their routines — mornings, bedtime, washing, dressing, the toilet. What they manage alone, and what they would like help with.
    • Eating and drinking: how food is prepared, any special cup or cutlery, how they like to be helped, what they actually like.
    • Glasses, hearing aids, teeth — anything that has to go on before the day can start properly.
    • What changes in an unfamiliar place, like a hospital ward?
    • Anything that sets them off, and what helps when it happens.

    The person, not the care. This is often the section professionals say they remember.

    • A short version of their life: where they grew up, what they did for a living, who mattered to them.
    • What they are like now — what they are good at, what they follow, what they will happily talk about for an hour.
    • What they would rather not talk about.
    • Anything else that did not fit above.

            Remove this person from the circle?

            They are the person who agrees to who joins. After this, you will need to ask somebody else.

            How?

            They drop out of the circle, and it comes off their device. Their own copy of Hearth does this. What they have already seen cannot be taken back.

            How it is different: removing somebody the ordinary way relies on their own Hearth to hide the circle and take it off their device. That works for almost everybody. But somebody using a changed copy of the app, or whoever has a stolen phone, could ignore it and go on seeing what is written.

            Moving does not rely on them at all. Hearth makes a new private circle and moves everybody else into it, and they are simply not in it — whatever app they use, they see nothing written from then on. They are not told.

            For serious cases: a stolen device, a safeguarding worry, or somebody who keeps writing.

            Anybody not using Hearth right now moves the next time they open it while you have it open too. The door address changes, so any invitation not yet used will need sending again.

            Everybody who moves will see this. The person removed never will.

            Inviting someone into the circle

            Send this to the person who will agree with you first. You chose that nobody joins this circle unless two of you say so. You choose who the second person is by pressing their name in Who is in this circle — so they have to be in it before you can ask them. Anybody you let in before that, you will have let in on your own.

            Everybody comes into this circle the same way: you give them its address, they paste it into their own Hearth, and you let them in.

            The address is the same for everybody and it never changes, so you only need to send it once, and you can send it to as many people as you like.

            It is safe to send. Having the address lets somebody ask to join. It does not let them in, it shows them nothing about them, and nobody gets in until you say so.
